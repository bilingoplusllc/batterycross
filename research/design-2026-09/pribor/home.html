One shell, three answers

The three silhouettes below are the same drawing, because the three cells are the same size. Only the needle moves. An instrument calibrated for 1.35 V mercury and fed a 1.50 V alkaline reads high across its whole range — the cell fits perfectly and the measurement is wrong.

LR44alkaline

Fits · in window The reference cell of this shell. Flat discharge curve until it is not, then a fast drop.

SR44silver oxide

Fits · in window 0.05 V higher and far steadier. The usual answer when an old meter wants stability rather than capacity.

MR44mercuric oxide

Fits · reads wrong 0.15 V below the window and withdrawn from the market. Devices built around it need an adapter or a recalibration, not a drop-in.

Method

Dimensions come from manufacturer datasheets and IEC designations, not from retail listings. A cell enters a family only when its diameter and height both match the envelope. Voltage classes are nominal open-circuit values; the interchange window is the family nominal with the tolerance that most compartments were designed around.

Limits

  • Fit is geometric. It says nothing about current, internal resistance or shelf life.
  • A cell inside the window can still be wrong for a device that was calibrated on a specific discharge curve.
  • Mercuric oxide cells are withdrawn in most markets. Adapters and recalibration are the honest answers, not a lookalike.
