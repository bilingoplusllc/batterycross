01

The specimen

LR44 is the reference cell of the 11.6 × 5.4 mm shell. It is alkaline, it is still made everywhere, and it is the cell most often stamped on the inside of a battery door that was designed forty years ago for something else.

Twelve other catalog entries share its envelope exactly, and two more share its diameter while falling 1.2 mm short of its height. The envelope is where a cross-reference starts. It is not where it ends: three chemistries with three different nominal voltages have been sold in this exact can, and one of them will make a calibrated instrument lie to you.

Below: the family at one scale, the 1.2 mm that separates a fit from a rattle, and the voltage picture.

03

The 1.2 mm

1.2 mm is the whole difference between LR43 and LR44, and it is invisible in a parts bin. The diameter matches, the cell slides in, the door closes, and nothing happens — or worse, it works intermittently while the spring contact bridges the gap under pressure and lets go when the case is knocked.

This is why the site never answers a fit question with a table of numbers alone. The red band above is the missing 1.2 mm, drawn at the same scale as the cell it belongs to. If you can see it, you do not have to compute it.

The reverse error exists too: a cell 1.2 mm proud of its envelope will hold the door open or deform the contact. Both directions are height clashes, and both are listed under near misses for this shell.

04

What fits is not what is right

The 11.6 × 5.4 mm can has carried three chemistries. Two of them land inside the interchange window and are interchangeable in nearly every consumer device. The third does not, and that is where the damage is done.

A meter calibrated on 1.35 V mercuric oxide and fed a 1.50 V alkaline reads high across its whole range — light meters, medical thermometers and older test gear all did this. The cell fits perfectly, the needle moves, and every reading is wrong by an amount that looks plausible. That is the failure this site exists to prevent.

Going the other way is milder but real: a device expecting 1.50 V and given 1.35 V starts life already near its low-battery threshold. 0.05 V — alkaline to silver oxide — is inside the window and is the safe swap, and silver oxide holds its voltage far flatter, which is why old instruments were often converted to it.

SR44+0.05 V

Drop-in Same envelope, inside the window, flatter discharge. The default answer when the compartment is 11.6 × 5.4 mm and the device cares about a steady reading.

MR44−0.15 V

Fits · reads wrong Withdrawn from the market. Devices designed around it need a voltage adapter or a recalibration — not a lookalike cell with the right diameter.

CR2032+1.50 V

Does not fit · wrong class 20.0 mm across and double the voltage. It will not enter this compartment, and if it did it would be a hazard, not a substitute.

06

Routes through this shell

Every discontinued entry carries a route. A route is not a claim that the two cells are the same; it is a statement of what to buy and what changes when you do.

303 SR44 / 357

Same cell, current designation. Nothing changes electrically.

MR44 SR44 + adapter

Fits without help, but the voltage rises by 0.20 V. A device calibrated on mercuric oxide needs a voltage-dropping adapter or a recalibration before its readings mean anything again.

LR43 / SR43 no route

1.2 mm short of this shell. They belong to the 11.6 × 4.2 mm family and have their own page; stacking or shimming them is not a substitution.

LR44 still supplied

No route needed. Sold under six designations; any of them is this cell.

Method

Diameter and height come from manufacturer datasheets and IEC designations. Silhouettes are generated from those two numbers at a single scale per drawing, with a millimeter rule in the same frame, so a drawing cannot disagree with the record it came from. Nominal voltage is the open-circuit value; the interchange window is the family nominal with the tolerance most compartments were designed around.

Limits

  • Fit is geometric only: it says nothing about current, internal resistance or shelf life.
  • A cell inside the window can still be wrong for a device calibrated on a specific discharge curve.
  • Mercuric oxide cells are withdrawn in most markets. The honest answer is an adapter or a recalibration, never a lookalike.
  • Nothing here is a safety certification. If a compartment shows corrosion or heat damage, stop.
