The same cell, other stampings

LR1154 is the IEC designation and 1166A the ANSI one; the rest are house codes. 357 and SR44 look identical on a shelf and are not the same cell — see section 03.

02

The socket it fills

An LR44 compartment is an 11.6 mm bore, 5.4 mm deep. Six cells are drawn into that same socket below, all at one scale. Three fill it, two leave daylight, one never gets in.

The 1.2 mm. Going the other way is the mistake that gets made: a device built around the 4.2 mm LR43 will not close on an LR44, because the lid has to travel 1.2 mm further than the case allows. The bore is right, the depth is not, and forcing the cover bends the contact.

Capacity is a nominal figure at a light continuous load. Alkaline button cells fall well short of it under pulsed loads, which is why watch and meter makers specified silver oxide instead.

04

What else this socket takes

Every cell below either fits this socket or is asked about often enough to be shown failing it. The bar is the distance from the 1.50 V the socket was designed around, measured from zero, in volts.

SR44, +0.05 V. Mechanically identical, and in a toy or a laser pointer the difference is nothing. In anything that measures, it is not nothing: silver oxide holds its 1.55 V almost flat until it dies, while this alkaline cell starts at 1.5 V and sags from the first hour. The gap between them is small at the start and grows for the rest of the cell's life.

PX675, −0.15 V. The mercury cell was built in this exact can, and that is the trap. A meter or a camera calibrated against its flat 1.35 V accepts an LR44 without complaint, then reads roughly two thirds of a stop off when the cell is new and a different amount every week after. PX675 has not been made since the mercury bans; the 1.4 V zinc-air 675 is the closest thing left, and it breathes.

Fitting is a fact about millimeters and it is drawn above. Working is a fact about your device, and this page describes it rather than certifying it. Check the manual before you trust a reading.

05

Cells LR44 is offered for

Sellers list LR44 against several dead codes. Two of those substitutions are honest and one is not.

Fits, reads low

Same can, same socket, wrong chemistry. Fine in a toy, wrong in the watch or meter that asked for 303 in the first place. The honest replacement for a 303 is a 357.

Drop-in

Both drawings agree: same shell, same chemistry, same nominal voltage, still made. There is nothing further to say about this one.

Will not fit

Both cells are drawn at the same scale here, which is the whole answer: LR44 rattles around inside a PX625 compartment and may never touch the contact. Adapters exist; a bare LR44 is not one.

07

Method

Both drawings on this page are generated from the 11.6 mm and 5.4 mm in the record, at the scale printed under them; nothing is adjusted for appearance. The socket outline is the bore and depth of the cell the page is named for, with no clearance drawn. Deviation is measured against that socket's own nominal voltage, from zero, on a scale that is broken where it says so. A cell counts as out of production when no listed manufacturer catalogs it.

Quick answer

If your device says LR44, A76 or AG13, buy LR44. If it says 357, SR44 or 303, buy 357 — the LR44 fits but reads 0.05 V low and falls from there.
