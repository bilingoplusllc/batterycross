Steel bar: within 50 mV, swap it. Orange bar: it fits and the reading moves.

Alkaline and silver oxide differ by 50 mV at the start and by far more at the end: the alkaline cell slopes down as it drains, the silver-oxide cell holds its voltage almost flat and then stops. A device that only has to run — a toy, a small light — will not notice. A device that measures will.

5.0 Clearance the miss that costs 1.2 mm

Compartment built for a 4.2 mm cell · 9 px = 1 mm

Same floor, same diameter, one lid. The orange band is the part that does not fit.

LR44 and LR43 share a diameter to the tenth of a millimeter, which is exactly why the mistake is common: the cell drops in, sits straight, makes contact. The lid is where it fails, and in a sealed case the lid is the part that cracks.

Δ against LR44 · a positive Δ mV means the device now sees more voltage than it was built for

If the instrument was designed around the 1.35 V mercury cell, no cell sold today is a true replacement. Silver oxide gets closest because its discharge curve is nearly flat, the property the mercury cell was chosen for, but it still starts 200 mV high. The honest options are a voltage-dropping adapter, a zinc-air cell at about 1.4 V with a short shelf life, or recalibration. This sheet will not pretend otherwise.

7.0 Method

Both drawings on this page come from the same two numbers as the table: 11.6 mm across, 5.4 mm tall. Nothing is illustrated by hand and nothing is resized to fill a column — when a figure is wider than the screen, the figure scrolls and the millimeters stay true.

Voltage is nominal open-circuit voltage for the chemistry. Capacity is a typical rating under a light continuous load at room temperature. Neither is a promise about the cell in your hand, and both are printed after the dimensions because dimensions are the only figures here that cannot be argued with.
